We’re in a weird time. Everyone’s got whiplash from the speed of progress.

Hard to understand today’s reality, let alone figure out how to react.

Why even attempt to predict the future? (clearly a fool’s errand)

Everyone needs a speculative designer scout mindset right now.

We can’t just give up or opt out because things are moving too fast. We need to think like fortune tellers and attempt to crystal ball.

Design engineers are the perfect people to figure out the shape of the future.

Uniquely positioned to prototype theories of the future in convincing ways.

Agents can “draw the rest of the owl” enough for you to make proof-of-concepts without full-stack skills (although they help).

Play with your ideas

Doing (cheesy) structured exercises to think deeply about your theories of the future and understand your assumptions.

04Play with your ideas
Invert itList what you’re assuming, then invert each one.
We prompt the agents
→ the agents prompt us
We build the software with agents
→ the software builds itself
Split itBreak the idea down into parts and list the attributes of the idea. Change one part at a time
OpenClaw: sandbox + memory + permissions + initiative.

Can the agent share memories within a team or family?
Can the agent tactfully forget sensitive facts or details?
Could an agent choose to delete evidence to protect its human?
04Play with your ideas
Maximise and minimisePlay out extreme versions of the future, in both directions.
Max: Running a thousand agents a day costs £1. What’s possible? Min: Agent intelligence becomes 10x more expensive. We have to ration. How do we adapt?
BrutethinkPick something unrelated and force it to match your idea.
Seasonal harvest: agent work happens in seasons. What kind of work maps to which season? Does your agent only pick what’s ripe?Postal service: how do agents mail each other? Who is the Royal Mail for agents?
04Play with your ideas
  • Max/min as a thought experiment. In tech, we love to maximise.
  • What if intelligence were free, infinite and ubiquitous?
  • Just as important: imagine futures for people outside the wealthy global North.
  • Scarcity; things going to shit. More interesting constraints to work with.

05artefacts from the future
  • Cricket-powder cereal: an artefact from the future.
  • Not enough animal protein for everyone — what if we ate insects? A big question a few years ago.
  • Insects are high in protein; climate change could limit how many animals we can keep.
  • Speculative design makes that future familiar and domestic: breakfast cereal.
  • Stay-at-home mums using OpenClaw for homeschooling and household organisation.
  • Historical parallel: early 20th-century women, vacuum cleaners, microwaves, washing machines. Less manual labour; more able to enter the workforce.
  • Debated, but I have the stats: this did happen.
  • My version: one kid, a house to renovate.
  • ChatGPT handles cognitive labour: next-size-up clothes, weekly meals, contractors, tracking home and family tasks.
  • Throwback adverts about that trend: love the retro look and the promise of agents.
  • A historical parallel with women, feminism and labour — not the trad wife dream. Some people on Twitter missed that.
  • Agents could relieve domestic cognitive labour, which falls more heavily on women.
  • The contrast: built mainly by single 20-year-olds in San Francisco with no domestic life.

Make your theories public and concrete

01

Use writing and conference talks to build theories. Write to clarify.

02

Tell people your theories. If it comes true, you get cred. If it doens’t no one remembers.

03

Back it up with data. The pressure of telling people and public debate will make you research it properly.

04Play with your ideas
  • We don’t do enough theory building in London.
  • Go to San Francisco and talk to people: everyone has theories.
  • Theory building is an important part of inventing the future.
  • Build a theory, test it against reality, then let what you learn shape the next theory.
